# Selling a company with several shareholders

*By [Joash Boyton, CEO of Acquiry](https://www.acquiry.com/joash-boyton/)*

A business owned by one person can be sold when that person decides to sell. A business with several shareholders needs those owners to agree, or needs a mechanism that allows a sale to proceed without unanimous agreement. Working out how that will happen before buyers are approached saves time and protects the process.

## Read the shareholders agreement

The shareholders agreement usually sets the rules. Look for:

- **Pre-emptive rights.** Existing shareholders may have the right to buy shares before they are offered to an outsider.
- **Drag rights.** Holders of a set majority may be able to require the others to sell on the same terms.
- **Tag rights.** Minority holders may be entitled to join a sale on the same terms as the majority.
- **Approval thresholds.** Certain decisions, including a sale, may need a special majority or board approval.
- **Deadlock provisions.** Some agreements set out what happens when owners cannot agree.

The company's constitution may contain further rules. Where documents conflict or are out of date, take legal advice before going to market.

## Align the owners early

Buyers lose confidence quickly if shareholders disagree in front of them. Before a process starts, owners should agree on:

1. Whether they want to sell, and in what timeframe
2. A minimum acceptable price and preferred structure
3. Who will lead negotiations and speak for the group
4. Which shareholders will stay on after completion, and on what terms
5. How proceeds will be split, including any preference shares or options

Different shareholders often want different things. A founder working in the business may accept an earn-out; a passive investor may want all cash at completion. These differences need to be resolved privately.

## Options and employee equity

Option holders and employees with shares need to be considered. Check how options vest on a sale and whether holders need to exercise before completion. Buyers will want a clean capitalisation table showing exactly who owns what.

## Warranties across several sellers

Buyers usually want warranties from all selling shareholders. Passive investors often resist giving the same warranties as managers who ran the business. Agreeing how warranty liability is shared, whether in proportion to shareholdings or otherwise, should happen before the sale agreement is negotiated.
