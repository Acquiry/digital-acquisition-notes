# Disclosure letters: how sellers qualify warranties in a business sale

*By Joash Boyton, CEO of Acquiry*

In most private acquisitions, the seller gives the buyer warranties: statements about the business that the seller promises are true. The accounts are accurate. There are no undisclosed disputes. The business owns its intellectual property. If a warranty turns out to be untrue, the buyer may have a claim.

The disclosure letter is the seller's main tool for managing that exposure. It sets out exceptions to the warranties, so that matters properly disclosed cannot later found a claim.

## How it works

The sale agreement typically says the warranties are given "subject to matters fairly disclosed" in the disclosure letter. The letter usually has two parts:

1. **General disclosures.** Categories of information treated as disclosed, such as public registers and the data room contents.
2. **Specific disclosures.** Matters set against particular warranties, for example a customer dispute disclosed against the litigation warranty.

## The standard of disclosure

Buyers and sellers often negotiate what counts as fair disclosure. Buyers want each matter described with enough detail to understand its nature and scope. Sellers prefer broad general disclosure, including the entire data room. A common middle ground is that a matter is disclosed if it is described in enough detail for a reasonable buyer to identify its nature and scope.

## Why buyers care

Disclosure shifts risk. A matter properly disclosed is usually the buyer's problem, not the seller's. That is why buyers review the disclosure letter closely and may respond with a price adjustment, a specific indemnity or a condition. Buyers who rely on warranty and indemnity insurance will also find that insurers generally exclude matters disclosed or known.

## Why sellers should start early

Preparing disclosures is a useful discipline. Going through each warranty forces the seller to identify issues before signing, when there is still time to explain or fix them. Sellers who do this as part of [exit](https://www.acquiry.com/exits/) preparation tend to have fewer surprises late in the process.

## Common mistakes

- Disclosing too late, so the buyer sees new issues in the final days before signing.
- Disclosing vaguely, so disclosure may not be effective.
- Disclosing inconsistently with the data room or the information memorandum.
- Forgetting to update disclosures between signing and completion when the deal has a gap.

## Where it fits

The disclosure letter is negotiated alongside the sale agreement, after diligence, in a structured [M&A process](https://www.acquiry.com/process/). Buyers running an [acquisition programme](https://www.acquiry.com/acquisitions/) should allow time to review it properly. Both sides benefit from grounding price discussions in evidence such as [sector multiples](https://www.acquiry.com/data/multiples/) and verified earnings from tools like the [SDE and EBITDA calculator](https://www.acquiry.com/tools/sde-ebitda-calculator/).
