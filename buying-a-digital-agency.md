# Buying a digital agency: what transfers with the clients, and what does not

By Joash Boyton, CEO of [Acquiry](https://www.acquiry.com/)

Digital agencies (marketing, development, design and similar service firms) are some of the most common small acquisitions, and some of the hardest to value well. Unlike a SaaS product or a content site, most of what an agency sells is the time and judgement of its people. A buyer needs to work out how much of the revenue belongs to the business, and how much belongs to individuals who may not stay.

## 1. Separate recurring work from project work

Split revenue into retainers, recurring contracts and one-off projects for at least the last 24 months. Retainers with notice periods are more predictable than a pipeline of projects, even if both show the same annual total. Ask for the contracts behind each retainer and check their terms, notice periods and renewal dates.

## 2. Measure client concentration

Rank clients by revenue and gross profit. Agencies often depend on a few large accounts. If one client represents a large share of profit, the buyer is effectively buying that relationship, and the price and structure should reflect the risk of losing it.

## 3. Find out who owns each relationship

For every major client, ask who the day-to-day contact is, who the client's decision maker deals with, and how long that relationship has lasted. If the founder is the relationship for most accounts, plan for a longer transition and consider tying part of the price to client retention.

## 4. Review contracts for change of control and assignment

Read client agreements for change of control clauses, assignment restrictions and termination for convenience. In an asset sale, contracts usually need to be assigned or novated, which may need each client's consent. A share sale may avoid assignment but can still trigger change of control rights.

## 5. Understand the team and how they are engaged

List every person who delivers work: employees, contractors and offshore partners. Check employment terms, restraints, accrued leave and entitlements, and whether contractors are properly classified. Ask which people are essential to key accounts, and talk with the seller early about retention arrangements.

## 6. Test delivery margin, not just revenue

Calculate gross margin by client and by service line, using actual hours and costs. Agencies can carry clients that look valuable on revenue but lose money once time is counted. Normalise the founder's salary and any unpaid work so earnings reflect what a new owner will actually inherit. Acquiry's [SDE and EBITDA calculator](https://www.acquiry.com/tools/sde-ebitda-calculator/) is a useful way to frame the adjustments.

## 7. Check work in progress and billing

Review unbilled work, prepaid retainers, overdue invoices and any disputes. Agree how work in progress and client prepayments will be treated at completion so neither side pays twice.

## 8. Structure for the risk

Because agency value sits with people and relationships, deals often use earn-outs tied to retained revenue or gross profit, a meaningful transition period for the founder, and retention arrangements for key staff. Compare the proposed price with [digital business valuation multiples](https://www.acquiry.com/data/multiples/) for service businesses, which are usually lower than for products with recurring software revenue.
