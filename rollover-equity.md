# Rollover equity: when the seller keeps a stake after the sale

*Buyer notes by Joash Boyton, CEO of [Acquiry](https://www.acquiry.com/joash-boyton/). General information only, not legal, tax or financial advice.*

In a rollover, the seller of a digital business takes part of the price as shares in the buyer, or in the company that will own the business, instead of cash. It is common where the buyer is a private equity backed platform, a holding company or a larger operator building through acquisitions. This note covers what both sides should settle before agreeing one.

## Why buyers propose it

- **Alignment.** A founder who still owns part of the business has a reason to support the transition and growth.
- **Less cash at completion.** The rolled portion does not need to be funded on day one.
- **A signal.** A seller willing to roll equity is saying they believe the business will keep performing under new ownership.

## Why sellers should be careful

The seller is swapping a certain amount of cash for a minority stake in a company they no longer control. The value of that stake depends on decisions they will not make, and they may not be able to sell it until the buyer chooses to exit.

## Terms to settle early

1. **What the seller rolls into.** Shares in the operating business, in a holding company, or in the buyer's wider group. Each carries different risk.
2. **Valuation of the new shares.** The seller should know the price per share they are receiving and how it was set, not just the headline percentage.
3. **Class of shares.** Ordinary shares alongside the investor, or a junior class behind preference shares. Preference terms can mean the seller receives little unless the exit is large.
4. **Information rights.** What financial reporting the seller will receive after completion.
5. **Exit and liquidity.** Tag-along rights if the majority owner sells, drag-along obligations, and whether there is any put option or agreed exit timetable.
6. **Leaver provisions.** What happens to the shares if the seller stops working in the business, and whether a good leaver and bad leaver distinction applies.
7. **Dilution.** How future capital raises or bolt-on acquisitions affect the seller's stake.
8. **Tax.** Whether rollover relief or a similar treatment is available in the relevant jurisdiction. This varies widely and needs specialist advice.

## How it interacts with price

A rollover is part of the consideration, so it belongs in the valuation discussion. If the cash price has been reduced in exchange for equity, both sides should be clear about what the equity is worth on the same basis. Benchmarks such as [digital business valuation multiples](https://www.acquiry.com/data/multiples/) help keep the cash and equity components on a consistent footing, and the [SDE and EBITDA calculator](https://www.acquiry.com/tools/sde-ebitda-calculator/) is a quick way to check the earnings figure the price rests on.

## In practice

Rollovers work best when the seller has a clear role after completion, the buyer has a credible plan for growth and exit, and the share terms are simple. They work worst when they are used to bridge a gap on price without either side being clear about what the stake is really worth.
