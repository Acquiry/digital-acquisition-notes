# Bridging a valuation gap between buyer and seller

*By Joash Boyton, CEO of Acquiry*

Many transactions stall on a single issue: the seller expects one number and the buyer offers another. A valuation gap is normal. It usually reflects different views about risk and the future rather than bad faith. The useful question is not who is right, but which part of the gap comes from disagreement about facts, which from disagreement about the future, and which from different appetites for risk.

## Separate the causes

**Different earnings base.** The two sides may be pricing different numbers. One uses adjusted earnings with generous add-backs, the other uses reported profit. Agreeing the earnings base first often closes much of the gap. The [SDE and EBITDA calculator](https://www.acquiry.com/tools/sde-ebitda-calculator/) is a simple way to lay out the adjustments side by side.

**Different multiple.** The seller may anchor on headline figures from larger or faster-growing businesses. Comparable data for businesses of similar size, growth and quality gives both sides a common reference. [Acquiry valuation multiples data](https://www.acquiry.com/data/multiples/) is a starting point.

**Different view of the future.** The seller sees a growth plan, the buyer sees execution risk. This is where structure helps most.

## Structures that close the gap

- **Deferred consideration.** Part of the price is paid later on fixed dates. The buyer conserves cash and the seller is paid in full if the business is sound.
- **Earn-out.** Part of the price depends on future performance. It rewards the seller if the plan is delivered, but needs clear measures and protections against manipulation.
- **Vendor finance.** The seller lends part of the price to the buyer. This signals confidence and widens the pool of buyers.
- **Rollover equity.** The seller keeps a stake and shares in future value.
- **Price adjustments tied to specific risks.** Where one identified risk drives the gap, a specific indemnity or escrow can address it more precisely than a lower headline price.

## When structure is not the answer

Structure adds complexity and continuing exposure. A seller who wants a clean break may prefer a lower price paid in full at completion. A buyer who doubts the business fundamentally should not paper over that doubt with an earn-out. The [Acquiry M&A process](https://www.acquiry.com/process/) shows how running a competitive process tests valuation expectations with several buyers at once.

## Practical steps

1. Agree the earnings base before debating the multiple.
2. Identify which risks drive the buyer's lower number.
3. Match each risk to a structure, or accept it in price.
4. Model what the seller actually receives under each proposal.
5. Keep the final structure as simple as the risks allow.
